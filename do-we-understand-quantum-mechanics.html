Does Anyone Really Understand Quantum Mechanics?

September 2026 · Mechanics / Quantum Mechanics

The mathematics of quantum mechanics is extraordinarily successful. Its predictions agree with experiments with astonishing precision. And yet, the question of what it actually means to ‘understand quantum mechanics’ remains far from settled.

In a 1964 lecture, physicist Richard P. Feynman famously remarked,

“I think I can safely say that nobody understands quantum mechanics.”

He did not mean that physicists cannot understand or use the mathematics of quantum mechanics. Rather, he was pointing to how difficult it is to develop an intuitive picture of quantum phenomena using the concepts and experiences of everyday life.

But there is an even deeper question.

Why does mathematics describe nature so remarkably well?

In 1960, physicist Eugene P. Wigner explored this strange question in his famous essay ‘The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences’. He described the extraordinary effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences as “something bordering on the mysterious.”

And it truly is remarkable.

Mathematicians have often developed mathematical structures for purely abstract reasons. Yet decades later, physicists have sometimes discovered that those very structures provide remarkably powerful descriptions of fundamental aspects of nature.

The use of mathematical structures such as Hilbert spaces in quantum mechanics is one striking example. Such mathematical ideas were developed long before their importance in physics became apparent.

So the question remains,

Is mathematics merely a language we have created to describe nature?

Or is there something deeper?

Do we use mathematics to describe the universe, or is the universe, in some sense, mathematical itself?
